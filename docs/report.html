01 — Hypothesis

Liquid leaders move first; illiquid followers catch up

The lead-lag hypothesis says that when common information arrives, the most liquid asset in a group prices it immediately while thinner names in the same group reprice with a delay. If that delay is long enough to observe and act on, it is tradable.

Solana is an unusually clean laboratory for this. SOL-USD turns over roughly $212bn a day and dominates its ecosystem's information flow. The tokens around it span five orders of magnitude of liquidity, from Render at $236m a day down to Star Atlas at under $1,000. One shared driver, a genuine liquidity ladder beneath it.

The system tests fourteen followers against that one leader, on hourly bars, over two years.

02 — Evidence

The effect is real, and it strengthens as liquidity falls

Correlating each follower's return at bar t with the leader's return at t−1 gives the lead-lag coefficient. The pattern is textbook: the coefficient rises monotonically as liquidity falls, while the contemporaneous correlation falls in step — the signature of stale prices absorbing information late rather than of simple co-movement.

Critically, the reverse direction is empty. No follower predicts the leader; the largest reverse coefficient anywhere in the basket is −0.026. The causality runs one way.

03 — Constraint

The signal lives where the volume does not

Every backtest so far has quietly assumed the orders could be filled. They cannot.

The number that ends the strategy

A $12,500 position in MOBILE-USD — a routine slice of a $100k book — is 74,000× that token's average hourly dollar volume. Samoyedcoin is 28,000×. Star Atlas is 15,000×. These are not assets with wide spreads; they are assets with essentially no volume at all.

Adding a capacity limit — no position may exceed 10% of an asset's average bar volume — is not a modelling nicety. It is the difference between a backtest and a trade. Once it is enforced, the four highest-signal names drop out of the book entirely, and they were carrying the result.

04 — Result

Removing the capacity limit is the only thing that creates an edge

Turning each component off in turn isolates what is actually doing the work. The Hawkes filter moves the result very little. The capacity limit moves it from nothing to everything — which is to say, the edge was never harvestable in the first place.

Against a round-trip cost of roughly 47bp, a gross edge of −0.14bp is not a marginal case. Breakeven would require costs near zero on assets whose spreads are among the widest in crypto.

The capacity curve confirms there is no book size that rescues it. Between $1,000 and $10m of gross notional, the gross edge never once separates from zero — the largest |t| anywhere on the curve is 1.04.

05 — Components

What the Hawkes filter and the neural net actually contributed

Hawkes crash filter — machinery sound, benefit did not replicate

The premise was promising. Negative jumps in these tokens cluster hard: conditional on a jump, the probability of another in the next bar runs 2.1× to 4.9× unconditional. Fitting λ(t) = μ + Σ α·exp(−β(t−tᵢ)) by maximum likelihood gives branching ratios from 0.48 to 0.89 — Render sits close to explosive, one shock from a nine-event cascade.

Early exploration suggested this split the loss-making tail cleanly, separating a +19bp regime from a −25bp one. It did not survive. That result used full-sample jump thresholds and a proxy specification chosen after looking at the data. Under trailing-only thresholds, properly fitted parameters and purged folds, the gate is roughly neutral — it helps only in the top 1% of leader moves, on 454 trades, at t = 1.15.

The estimator itself is correct: it recovers known branching ratios from simulated processes to within 0.01. The trading benefit was selection bias, and the inference layer was built precisely to catch that.

Self-adapting neural overlay — a small, honest nothing

A regime-gated mixture of experts predicts the residual the linear baseline misses, adapting online through a replay buffer that mixes recent bars with a reservoir sample of history. Its blend weight is earned from realised out-of-sample information coefficient, so a net that adds nothing collapses to the baseline on its own.

Across 19 folds it achieved a mean IC of +0.023, positive in 12 of them, lifting gross Sharpe from 0.49 to 0.66 and the mean trade from −0.98bp to −0.14bp. That is a real but statistically insignificant improvement on a strategy that remains firmly unprofitable. The CUSUM regime detector fired zero times — validated at 47 detections on synthetic regime breaks, it simply found none here.

Verdict

A real inefficiency, protected by its own illiquidity

The honest reading is not that the hypothesis was wrong. The lead-lag effect is present, strongly significant, and correctly ordered by liquidity. It is simply located exclusively in assets too thin to trade — which is the most likely reason it still exists. An effect this visible in a liquid asset would have been arbitraged away years ago.
