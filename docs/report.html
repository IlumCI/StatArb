01 — Hypothesis

Liquid leaders move first; illiquid followers catch up

The lead-lag hypothesis says that when common information arrives, the most liquid asset in a group prices it immediately while thinner names in the same group reprice with a delay. If that delay is long enough to observe and act on, it is tradable.

Two markets were tested independently, chosen to fail in different ways.

Solana — SOL-USD turns over roughly $212bn a day and dominates its ecosystem's information flow. The fourteen tokens around it span five orders of magnitude of liquidity, from Render at $236m a day down to Star Atlas at under $1,000. Continuously traded, 17,477 hourly bars.

US equities — IWM (Russell 2000) leading twenty-seven small and micro caps, screened on liquidity and data coverage alone and never on measured lead-lag. Seven bars per session across 730 sessions. A much narrower liquidity ladder, but every name trades real volume.

The second market is the control. If the Solana result were a crypto artefact, equities would not reproduce it.

02 — Evidence

The effect is real, and it strengthens as liquidity falls

Correlating each follower's return at bar t with the leader's return at t−1 gives the lead-lag coefficient. In Solana the pattern is textbook: the coefficient rises monotonically as liquidity falls, while the contemporaneous correlation falls in step — the signature of stale prices absorbing information late rather than of simple co-movement. Reverse causality is empty; the largest reverse coefficient in the basket is −0.026.

Equities reproduce the effect at greater strength — 19 of 27 names at t > 2, mean coefficient +0.051 against Solana's +0.036 — but with two differences that matter. The gradient is flat: the liquid half averages +0.047 and the illiquid half +0.054, so unlike crypto the signal is not concentrated where it cannot be traded. And reverse causality is present but small, +0.019 against a forward +0.051, so the equity causality is real but less clean.

03 — Where it goes

The catch-up completes before you can trade it

A lead-lag coefficient measured on closing prices answers the wrong question. It asks whether the follower moves after the leader — but a strategy cannot transact at the close it used to form the signal. It sees the close of bar t, and the earliest it can buy is the open of bar t+1.

Splitting the follower's next-bar move into those two legs is decisive, and both markets give the same answer: nearly all of the predictable move lands in the gap between the two prices.

This is the whole result, and it is market independent. The effect is not an illusion: the follower really does reprice after the leader. It reprices in the auction and the first print of the next bar, where a strategy watching bar closes has no way to participate.

That also explains why the inefficiency survives. It is not protected by obscurity or by risk. It is protected by the fact that capturing it requires being in the queue before the print, which is a different business from the one this system is in.

04 — Constraint

In crypto, the signal lives where the volume does not

Beyond the gap problem, the Solana basket has a second, independent defect: its backtest quietly assumed the orders could be filled. They cannot.

The number that ends the strategy

A $12,500 position in MOBILE-USD — a routine slice of a $100k book — is 74,000× that token's average hourly dollar volume. Samoyedcoin is 28,000×. Star Atlas is 15,000×. These are not assets with wide spreads; they are assets with essentially no volume at all.

Adding a capacity limit — no position may exceed 10% of an asset's average bar volume — is not a modelling nicety. It is the difference between a backtest and a trade. Once it is enforced, the four highest-signal names drop out of the book entirely, and they were carrying the result: the measured edge falls from +8.50bp to −0.14bp.

Equities do not have this problem. The thinnest name in that basket still turns over $51k per bar, and the lead-lag gradient there is flat — the liquid half averages +0.047 against the illiquid half's +0.054. Removing the capacity limit moves the equity edge only from +1.39bp to +2.15bp. That is the control working: the capacity story is specific to crypto, while the gap story is not.

05 — Result

Two markets, two failure modes, one verdict

Turning each component off in turn isolates what is actually doing the work. In Solana the Hawkes filter moves the result very little while the capacity limit moves it from nothing to everything. In equities capacity is nearly irrelevant and there was never much edge to remove.

06 — Components

What the Hawkes filter and the neural net actually contributed

Hawkes crash filter — machinery sound, benefit did not replicate

The premise was promising. Negative jumps in these tokens cluster hard: conditional on a jump, the probability of another in the next bar runs 2.1× to 4.9× unconditional. Fitting λ(t) = μ + Σ α·exp(−β(t−tᵢ)) by maximum likelihood gives branching ratios from 0.48 to 0.89 — Render sits close to explosive, one shock from a nine-event cascade.

Early exploration suggested this split the Solana loss-making tail cleanly, separating a +19bp regime from a −25bp one. It did not survive. That result used full-sample jump thresholds and a proxy specification chosen after looking at the data. Under trailing-only thresholds, properly fitted parameters and purged folds, the gate is roughly neutral — it helps only in the top 1% of leader moves, on 454 trades, at t = 1.15.

The estimator itself is correct: it recovers known branching ratios from simulated processes to within 0.01. The trading benefit was selection bias, and the inference layer was built precisely to catch that.

Self-adapting neural overlay — earns a little in crypto, nothing in equities

A regime-gated mixture of experts predicts the residual the linear baseline misses, adapting online through a replay buffer that mixes recent bars with a reservoir sample of history. Its blend weight is earned from realised out-of-sample information coefficient, so a net that adds nothing collapses to the baseline on its own.

On Solana it achieved a mean IC of +0.023 across 19 folds, positive in 12, lifting gross Sharpe from 0.49 to 0.66. On equities the same net achieved a mean IC of −0.005, positive in only 5 of 11 folds — actively worse than nothing. In both cases the blend behaved exactly as designed: it bought a little of the Solana net and essentially none of the equity one.

That asymmetry is the useful part. A net that reported a positive contribution on both markets would be suspicious; one that earns a small weight where there is residual structure and declines it where there is not is behaving like a measuring instrument rather than a slot machine. The CUSUM regime detector fired zero times in either market — validated at 47 detections on synthetic regime breaks, it simply found none here.

Verdict

A real inefficiency, protected by the moment it happens in

The hypothesis was not wrong. The lead-lag effect is present in both markets, strongly significant, correctly signed, and in crypto correctly ordered by liquidity. What the two markets together establish is why it is not tradable, and the answer is not the one the crypto sample alone suggested.

Crypto pointed at capacity: the edge sat in tokens nobody can fill. Equities removed that explanation — a flat liquidity gradient, real volume, half the costs — and the strategy still failed. What survived both tests is the gap. The follower reprices between the close and the next open, and a system that decides on closes is structurally one print too late.

  • ✓Lead-lag structure confirmed in both markets: 10/14 Solana followers and 19/27 equity followers at t > 2, with correctly signed, near-zero reverse causality.
  • ✓Hawkes estimator validated against simulated processes; branching ratios well differentiated across both baskets.
  • ✓The failure mechanism is identified and reproduces across two unrelated markets: the predictable move lands in the untradeable close-to-open gap.
  • ✗No tradable edge at any book size from $1,000 to $10m in either market. Largest |t| on either capacity curve is 1.04.
  • ✗Hawkes trading benefit did not replicate out of sample once full-sample thresholds were removed.
  • ✗Deflated Sharpe ratio 0.000 on every configuration in both markets — indistinguishable from selection luck.

Where this would have to go next is a different data regime, not a different model: quote-level data, entry at the touch rather than the next bar's open, and an execution path that can post inside the gap. Nothing in the residual suggests a bar-close strategy can be rescued by a better forecast.
